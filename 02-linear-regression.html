Part 01

Purpose and route

This activity develops linear regression from an intuitive line through a scatterplot to a complete modelling workflow. You will calculate coefficients, inspect residuals, interpret uncertainty, extend a model with groups and interactions, and distinguish in-sample fit from predictive performance.

Allow 100–130 minutes, or use two sessions. Parts 2–6 form the core OLS route. Parts 7–10 extend it to multiple regression, diagnostics, transformations and prediction. R work is separate.

Your final product is a short model report with a numerical result, a substantive interpretation, a diagnostic finding and an honest evaluation plan.

Part 03

Fit the line by least squares

Move the intercept and slope to find a line with a small sum of squared residuals (SSE). The dashed segments show vertical residuals. Squaring prevents positive and negative residuals from cancelling and penalises large misses strongly.

b₁ = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) / Σ(x − x̄)²
b₀ = ȳ − b₁x̄

Here x̄ = 4, ȳ = 60, Sxx = 10 and Sxy = 52. With an intercept, the least-squares line passes through (x̄, ȳ), and the fitted residuals sum to zero.

Part 04

Variation, R-squared and residual error

For OLS with an intercept, total variation around the outcome mean splits into variation in fitted values and residual variation: SST = SSR + SSE. In this example SST = 278, SSE = 7.6 and SSR = 270.4.

R² = 1 − SSE / SST     s = √[SSE / (n − 2)]

R² measures how much of the sample variation is represented by the fitted line. The residual standard error s measures residual size in outcome units. Neither is automatically a statement about a future population.

R² = 0.973 proves that study hours cause 97.3% of the score.

Part 05

Coefficient uncertainty and testing

A fitted coefficient changes across samples. Under the classical independent Normal, constant-variance error model, the slope standard error is s/√Sxx and a 95% confidence interval is b₁ ± tn−2,.975 SE(b₁).

For the five observations: SE(b₁) = 0.5033; the critical t value with 3 degrees of freedom is 3.182; the slope interval is [3.598, 6.802]. Testing a zero slope gives t = 10.331 and a two-sided p-value of 0.00193. The small sample makes the assumptions especially consequential.

These calculations do not require the predictor itself to be Normally distributed. A p-value is not the probability that the null is true; a confidence interval is not a list of values with posterior probabilities assigned by this frequentist procedure.

Part 06

Predict a mean or a new individual

At five study hours, the line predicts 65.2 points. An interval for the mean score at five hours describes uncertainty in the conditional mean. A prediction interval for a new individual score also includes individual variation around that mean, so it is wider.

Both kinds of intervals widen away from the centre of the observed predictor values. The prediction at eight hours is 80.8, but eight lies outside the observed range of two to six hours: the linear relation may not continue there.

Part 07

Multiple regression, groups and interactions

Multiple regression includes several predictors. A coefficient describes an association conditional on the other included predictors. A categorical predictor should be represented through explicit reference categories rather than arbitrary numerical spacing.

E(Y | x,D) = b₀ + b₁x + b₂D + b₃xD

For D = 0, the intercept is b₀ and slope is b₁. For D = 1, the intercept is b₀ + b₂ and slope is b₁ + b₃. The group difference at x is b₂ + b₃x. In the widget, b₀ = 40 and b₁ = 4 remain fixed.

Dark: D = 0. Orange: D = 1. These are illustrative equations, not estimates from a dataset.

Part 08

Outliers, leverage and influence

An observation can be unusual vertically (a large residual), unusual in predictor space (high leverage), or influential because including it changes the fitted model. These concepts overlap but are different. A high-leverage observation lying near the existing line need not pull the line strongly.

Add one point to the five-observation dataset. Compare the new fitted line with the original grey line. Try a point near the old line at x = 10, then a point with the same x and a much lower y.

A large Cook’s distance means you should automatically delete the observation.

Part 09

Diagnostics and transformations

Plot residuals against fitted values. A curved pattern can indicate a misspecified mean; a fan-shaped spread can suggest heteroskedasticity. Check leverage and influence, and consider dependence between observations. An apparently pattern-free plot does not prove every assumption.

Heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors such as HC3 change the uncertainty estimate, not the OLS line. They do not repair an omitted-variable problem, a wrong mean model or unaddressed clustering.

Logs require positive values. Exponentiating a fitted log outcome does not generally recover the arithmetic conditional mean. The R lab illustrates a smearing correction estimated from training residuals, with a note about its assumptions.

Robust standard errors fix bias due to an omitted confounder.

Part 10

Prediction and model comparison

Adding predictors to nested OLS models cannot increase training SSE. Consequently, training R² cannot fall, even if the new predictor contributes no useful information about future outcomes. Adjusted R² penalises model size but remains an in-sample criterion.

The housing data contain 180 fictional records and a predefined split. Compare an area-only model, a model adding renovation and location, and a model adding an area-by-centre interaction. Select a procedure using validation or development-set cross-validation, then evaluate on the predefined test set.

Report RMSE and MAE in thousands of euros, together with a training-derived baseline. Avoid choosing the most favourable model after inspecting test errors. If a model is selected for inference rather than prediction, recognise that data-driven selection affects ordinary inferential statements.
