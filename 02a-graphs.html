What do these records measure?

Measurement, variable types and the unit of analysis

Our file contains 24 fictional firms, with one filing per firm for a single reference year. Each row represents a firm and its filing, not an invoice or an individual taxpayer.

VariableMeaningType

filing_delay_daysNonnegative days late, 0 = on timeQuantitative, discrete, ratio scale

An ordered risk band would be ordinal. Celsius temperature is an example of an interval scale: differences have meaning, but ratios depend on the chosen zero.

Compare your reasoning

Identifiers are arbitrary labels, so their numerical average has no substantive meaning. In an invoice-level dataset, firms with more invoices contribute more rows. An average across invoices and an average across firms answer different questions.

Validity: does a measure capture the intended concept? Filing delay measures timeliness, not every aspect of compliance. Reliability: would the same rule applied to the same records give consistent results? Consistent measurement can still miss the intended concept.
Frequencies, denominators and missing values
From E-tivity 1: district A pays 120 of 160 assessed units (75%); B pays 36 of 40 (90%). The combined ratio is 156/200 = 78%, not the unweighted mean 82.5%. More revenue and a higher payment ratio are different results. This is a separate illustrative scenario from the 24-firm dataset below.

Counts and proportions

For category j, pj = nj / n. Proportions sum to 1 when the categories cover the denominator.

Unknown is not zero

Two delay values are missing. Zero means a known on-time filing. Audit outcomes are only observed when audited = 1.

Worked answer

18 filings are known to be late and 22 have a known delay: 18/22 = 81.8% among observed delays. The fraction 18/24 = 75% is a lower bound for the full set, not an assumption that the missing records were on time. If both missing records were late, the full proportion would be 20/24 = 83.3%.

The same values can tell different visual stories

Illustrative annual tax receipts rise from 9.8 to 10.5 million monetary units. Before moving the slider, calculate the increase.

The change is always 0.7 million, about 7.14%. Bar length normally needs a zero baseline. A clearly labelled restricted axis may be appropriate for a line or dot plot, where full bar length is not the encoding.

VariableUseful displayKeep this information
Nominal: sectorBarsDistinct categories and a common baseline
Ordinal: satisfactionOrdered barsThe natural order from low to high
Discrete: 0, 1, 2, 5, 10 casesSticks or dotsActual numerical distances
Continuous: processing timeHistogram or boxplotDistribution shape and measurement units
Three frequent mistakes
  • One bar per distinct measurement: when most values occur once, equal-height bars hide the distribution. Use a dotplot or suitable bins.
  • Counts presented as rates: 120 payments and 36 payments say nothing about the fraction due without denominators. Recall the district ratios 75% and 90%.
  • A pie chart for multiple responses: among 100 taxpayers, 65 used online help, 45 telephone help and 30 a service desk. The 140 selections overlap. Show the three percentages as bars with denominator 100; do not normalise them into slices of a fictitious whole. A pie requires mutually exclusive parts of one total. Many similar slices are hard to compare even when valid.

E-TIVITY 2A · SECTION 4 / 6

Intervals, density and grouped observations

Use a bar chart for categories. Use a histogram to show the distribution of a numerical variable. Bin choice changes the visible detail.

Histogram of the 24 synthetic turnover values. The last bin includes its right endpoint.

Unequal intervals

When widths differ, height must be a frequency density so that area represents count (or relative frequency).

height = count / bin width
Interpreting this distribution

Turnover has a long right tail, including values 120 and 300. A large number of bins can create empty intervals and visual noise in a small dataset. Wide bins hide local detail. In unequal-width histograms, count heights would give wide bins disproportionate area.

The modal interval uses density when widths differ
IntervalCountWidthDensity
[0,10)18101.80
[10,15)1252.40
[15,30]20151.33

The largest count is in [15,30], but the greatest concentration per unit of width is in [10,15). This is the modal interval of the density histogram.

A median estimated from grouped data

Processing times have intervals [0,12), [12,24), [24,36), [36,48] hours and counts 8, 15, 12, 5. Cumulative counts are 8, 23, 35, 40. The 50% point lies in [12,24).

Median ≈ 12 + [(20 − 8) / 15] × 12 = 21.6 hours

This linear interpolation assumes a uniform spread within the interval. The raw-data median is not known exactly, and the class midpoint 18 is not automatically the median.

E-TIVITY 2A · SECTION 5 / 6

From analysis to decision

A dashboard headline says: “Receipts have surged, and only 75% of firms filed late.” Receipts moved from 9.8 to 10.5 million. In the 24-firm file, 18 delays are positive and two delays are missing.

Compare with a worked response

Receipts increased by 0.7 million, about 7.14%. A zero-baseline bar chart displays the size of this difference without exaggerating bar lengths. Of the 22 firms with known delays, 18 filed late (81.8%). Among all 24 firms, the late proportion lies between 75% and 83.3%. We cannot rank office performance without comparable definitions, periods and case coverage.
