Part 01

Purpose and preparation

Logistic regression models the probability of a binary event. This activity moves from probabilities to odds and log-odds, then connects fitted probabilities with decisions, error costs and evaluation on new data.

Allow 90–120 minutes. Prerequisites are proportions, conditional probability and basic regression interpretation. Complete the browser activity without R; the linked R session then fits models and evaluates them on supplied datasets.

Your final product is a short recommendation for a prediction-based support programme, including an explicit target, threshold rationale, labelled confusion matrix and a limitation.

Part 02

Define the event and the probability

For a delivery, define Y = 1 if it arrives after its promised deadline and Y = 0 otherwise. The model estimates P(Y = 1 | X), the event probability conditional on the available predictors. Be explicit about the event: a probability of 0.8 means an 80% predicted chance of the outcome coded as 1.

A linear probability model can yield predictions outside [0,1]. Logistic regression uses a link function that keeps probabilities in that range. A binary target also has conditional variance p(1 − p), which varies with its mean.

Part 03

Probability, odds and log-odds

odds = p / (1 − p)
logit(p) = log[p / (1 − p)]
p = odds / (1 + odds)

Probability compares event outcomes with all outcomes. Odds compare event outcomes with non-event outcomes. If p = 0.20, the odds are 0.20/0.80 = 0.25, or one event for every four non-events in a probability interpretation.

Part 05

Coefficients, odds ratios and conditional effects

For a one-unit increase in a predictor with no interaction, the odds multiply by exp(b₁), holding other predictors fixed. A coefficient of 0.18 corresponds to an odds ratio of 1.197. A peak coefficient of 0.9 corresponds to an odds ratio of 2.460.

Probability effects are nonlinear. With a simple continuous predictor, the derivative is b₁p(1 − p); the same coefficient changes probability more near 0.5 than near the extremes. For binary predictors, calculate the probability difference between 0 and 1.

Adjusted odds ratios condition on the included variables. They need not equal crude odds ratios. With an interaction xD, the odds ratio for D can depend on x. Conditional associations alone are not estimates of causal intervention effects.

Part 06

Fitting and uncertainty

Logistic regression is typically fitted by maximum likelihood. Each positive observation contributes p and each negative contributes 1 − p to the likelihood. The fitting algorithm seeks coefficients that make the observed outcomes jointly plausible, or equivalently minimise log loss.

The R laboratory fits late ~ distance_km + peak on the 300 training deliveries. It estimates approximately −3.4315 for the intercept, 0.2101 for distance and 0.9463 for peak time. These differ from the simulation coefficients because a finite random sample was observed.

Wald intervals use the coefficient estimate and its standard error. Exponentiating interval endpoints yields an interval for an odds ratio. These approximations can fail with sparse data, very rare outcomes or separation. If a predictor perfectly separates events from non-events, ordinary maximum-likelihood coefficients can diverge.

Thresholds and the confusion matrix

A model supplies probabilities. A decision rule supplies a threshold: predict event = 1 when p ≥ c. Lowering the threshold labels more cases positive; this can recover more events and also create more false alarms. Probabilities and fitted coefficients remain unchanged.

Part 08

Error costs and choosing a threshold

Suppose a false positive costs one unit and a false negative costs four. At threshold 0.50, the fixed example has TP = 4, FP = 1, FN = 2 and TN = 5. Its accuracy is 0.75 but its total error cost is 1 + 4 × 2 = 9. At threshold 0.30, accuracy is lower (0.667) but cost is only 7.

If probabilities are calibrated and the only costs are constant false-positive and false-negative costs, the idealised rule predicts positive when p ≥ CFP/(CFP + CFN). Costs 1 and 4 imply 0.20. This theoretical rule is not identical to minimising realised cost on a small validation sample; capacity limits or effects of the intervention can change the decision problem.

Part 09

Ranking, calibration and probability scores

The ROC curve plots recall against the false-positive rate as the threshold changes. In the 12-case exercise, AUC is 0.7778. AUC measures how often a randomly chosen positive receives a higher score than a randomly chosen negative, with half credit for ties. It is not accuracy at a particular threshold.

Calibration asks whether predicted chances agree with event frequencies. Among cases assigned probabilities around 0.20, events should occur around 20% of the time in a calibrated model. Compare average probabilities with observed rates and display the number of cases in each group.

Brier score = mean[(y − p)²]
Log loss = −mean[y log(p) + (1 − y) log(1 − p)]

Lower scores are better under these probability losses. Compare against a constant probability learned from the training event rate. For rare outcomes, inspect precision and recall as well as ROC, and report the event count. A high AUC can coexist with poor probability calibration or unacceptable error costs.

Part 10

Late filing and the meaning of labels

The tax-compliance case contains 600 simulated records. The target is late_filing. The predictors describe previous late filings, an ordinal complexity score and support used before the deadline. The digital-channel indicator is reserved for an illustrative comparison of test performance across groups.

The simulation contains an outcome for every record. Real data may instead contain outcomes observed only after a selected audit or follow-up. Such selection can make the labelled sample different from the population where predictions will be used. A model trained on selected cases does not automatically estimate population risk.

Late filing is not a finding of deliberate evasion or fraud. A probability should be interpreted in relation to its target and data. Group error comparisons with few cases are uncertain; one table does not establish fairness. Consider review, correction and access to support in the proposed decision process.
