SECTION 03 / 10

The sampling distribution describes estimates

Each repeated draw produces one mean and one proportion. The histograms below contain 500 estimates, not 500 individual filings. They use the sample size and selection rule chosen in the previous section.

SE(x̄) = σ / √n × √[(N−n)/(N−1)]
SE(p̂) = √[π(1−π)/n] × √[(N−n)/(N−1)]

These exact finite-register standard errors apply to simple random sampling without replacement from all N records; σ uses divisor N. For independent draws with replacement, omit the finite-population factor.

Why the count is not exactly binomial here

Sampling without replacement from a finite register gives a hypergeometric discrepancy count. A binomial model applies to independent Bernoulli trials, or approximates this design when the sampling fraction is small. For an approximately normal proportion, expected numbers in both categories should be sufficiently large.

SECTION 04 / 10

More selected data can still miss the target

Use “Only from the 250 largest declared amounts”, then repeat the simulations. Compare the histogram centre with the full-register target line.

A larger sample reduces random variation within the selected group. It does not supply information about excluded firms. A targeted sample can answer a question about the targeted group, but its unweighted mean generally does not estimate the whole-register mean.
PropertyMeaning
UnbiasednessAcross repetitions under the specified design/model, the estimator is centred on the parameter.
ConsistencyAs information grows under suitable conditions, the estimator converges in probability to the parameter. It may still be biased at finite n.
EfficiencyCompare variance among appropriate competing estimators, holding the target and assumptions fixed.
Compare your reasoning

The audit may estimate the outcome among high-priority cases more precisely. Generalisation to all filings requires an appropriate design or justified weighting/model assumptions. Larger n alone does not remove selection bias.

SECTION 05 / 10

Estimate ± uncertainty

The supplied fictional sample has 25 independent processing times. Its mean is 11.4 days and sample SD is about 2.3805 days. The illustrative benchmark of 10 days is not a legal deadline.

σ known: x̄ ± z1−α/2 σ/√n
σ unknown: x̄ ± t1−α/2,n−1 s/√n

The z formula is exact for independent normal observations with known σ; the t formula is exact for independent normal observations with unknown σ. Large-sample approximations require suitable conditions, not a universal n = 30 rule.

Changing the controls creates hypothetical summary data; the downloadable sample remains unchanged.

Reading t and degrees of freedom

For n = 25, df = 24. A two-sided 95% interval uses the 0.975 quantile, about 2.0639, leaving 0.025 in each tail. If a table labels upper-tail probabilities, use column 0.025. Estimating the mean imposes one constraint on deviations, leaving n−1 degrees of freedom.

SECTION 06 / 10

What does 95% refer to?

Here we draw independent normal observations from a teaching model with μ = 10 days and σ = 2 days. We estimate σ from each sample and construct t intervals.

Dark blue intervals contain μ; orange intervals marked × miss it. The vertical line is μ = 10. The display shows all 100 intervals.

Compare your reasoning

Under the assumptions, 95% of intervals from this procedure cover the fixed mean in the long run. A realised interval either covers it or does not. 95% is not the fraction of individual processing times inside the interval and is not a posterior probability for the fixed parameter.

SECTION 07 / 10

A test compares data with a reference model

  1. Specify the population, parameter, null hypothesis and alternative.
  2. Choose α and the test direction before inspecting the result.
  3. Check the design and model; calculate the statistic and p-value.
  4. Report the estimate, uncertainty and practical meaning.

For the processing sample: H₀: μ = 10 days. Use the mean, SD and n from the interval calculator.

t = (x̄ − μ₀)/(s/√n),   df = n−1
The test here uses the unknown-σ t formulation, regardless of the interval method selector. A p-value is a tail probability under H₀ and the model, for a statistic at least as extreme in the prespecified direction. It is not P(H₀ is true).
Worked conclusion

x̄ = 11.4; difference = 1.4 days; t = 2.9406 with 24 df; p = 0.0071. Reject H₀ at 5%. The 95% interval for μ is [10.4174, 12.3826], and for μ−10 is [0.4174, 2.3826] days. This is evidence of a mean difference under the sampling/model assumptions. It does not identify a cause or prove that a change is operationally important.

SECTION 08 / 10

A discrepancy proportion has its own standard error

A separate hypothetical audit uses independent Bernoulli observations: 38 discrepancies in 200 cases. This is a teaching model, not the finite-register sample above. Test H₀: π = 0.15 against π ≠ 0.15.

z = (p̂−π₀) / √[π₀(1−π₀)/n]

The test uses the variance under H₀. The displayed 95% interval is the Wilson score interval, which avoids the zero-width boundary problem of the basic Wald interval. The uncorrected two-sided score test rejects at 5% precisely when π₀ falls outside that Wilson interval, apart from rounding.

Large-sample checks and the simpler Wald formula

Check nπ₀ and n(1−π₀); here both exceed 10. The interface flags smaller counts. For sparse data, an exact binomial procedure may be preferable. The basic Wald interval p̂ ± 1.96√[p̂(1−p̂)/n] is a common introduction but can have poor coverage, especially near 0 or 1. These procedures assume independent trials with a common probability.
