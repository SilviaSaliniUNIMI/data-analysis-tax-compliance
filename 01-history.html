SECTION 01 / 8

Why did governments need to count?

Who owes tax, on what basis, and how much is actually paid?

Fiscal administration is an important root of systematic data collection. Registers of people, land and property helped authorities identify resources and allocate obligations. This activity connects that history to contemporary tax data and human decisions.

Two different questions. Tax assessment determines an obligation under a set of rules. Tax compliance concerns meeting those obligations, including registration, reporting and payment. We use this distinction to interpret the historical examples; their institutions and rules were different from those of today.

This is a dedicated activity for this course. Allow 60–90 minutes: historical reading (20), primary-source discussion (15), numerical exploration (15), quiz and reflection (20–30).

  • Explain why a register is created and whom it includes.
  • Distinguish a measurement from a policy choice.
  • Compare revenue amounts with payment ratios.
  • Connect data coverage to the limits of a predictive model.

SECTION 02 / 8

From people and property to public knowledge

These milestones are a selective pathway, not a complete history. Administrative counting is older than modern probability-based statistical inference.

Roman censuses · people, property and obligations

Registers of citizens and their property supported public administration, including taxation and civic obligations. The recorded population was defined by the institutions collecting the information; it should not be assumed to match a modern inclusive population census.

Source: Office for National Statistics, Census-taking in the ancient world ↗.

1086 · the Domesday survey

William I ordered a detailed survey of landholding and resources in England. Valuations and information about who held land helped the Crown understand its fiscal resources. Domesday was not a census of every person.

Ask: what could a property register reveal that a headcount could not?

Sources: The National Archives, Domesday Book guide ↗; historical context ↗.

1662 · Petty and taxation

In A Treatise of Taxes and Contributions, William Petty examined ways to raise public revenue. His discussion of poll taxes exposes a problem that counting alone cannot solve: simple administration does not establish an equitable allocation of tax burdens.

Primary source: Petty, A Treatise of Taxes and Contributions ↗, chapter VII.

1662 · Graunt and mortality

In the same year, John Graunt published his observations on the Bills of Mortality. Studying deaths and population provides a parallel thread: the development of statistical reasoning was not confined to taxation.

Primary source: Graunt, Natural and Political Observations … upon the Bills of Mortality ↗ (Wellcome Collection).

1826 · Gioia and statistical knowledge

Melchiorre Gioia (also catalogued as Gioja) published Filosofia della statistica in Milan in 1826. We use his work as a bridge to a wider question: how can organised observations about society inform public decisions?

Course connection: a useful tax dataset needs clear definitions and an explicit purpose. This is our present-day application, not a claim that Gioia proposed modern tax-risk models.

Sources: bibliographic record ↗; digitised historical volume ↗.

Fiscal records are one historical thread. Demography, probability and scientific inquiry also contributed to modern statistics. Avoid the claim that statistics was invented only for taxation.

SECTION 03 / 8

Counting people does not choose a fair tax

Open Petty’s 1662 treatise ↗ and locate chapter VII, “Of Poll-money”, paragraphs 2–6. The spelling is historical. Focus on the argument rather than every unfamiliar word.

Reading guide (paraphrase). Petty questions rank or title as a measure of a person’s resources. He notes the unequal burden of charging unequal people the same amount, while recognising the administrative convenience of counting people to estimate revenue.
  1. Which characteristics might be easy to record?
  2. Which would be relevant to ability to pay?
  3. Why can those two sets of characteristics differ?

Read this as a historical argument in its seventeenth-century political setting, not as a statement of current tax law or an endorsement of all the author’s positions.

After writing: compare the reasoning

A headcount can support a fixed charge per person, but cannot describe differences in wealth or income. A rank can be easy to classify without accurately measuring resources. Data describe consequences of alternative rules; a rule also requires a political or normative choice.

SECTION 05 / 8

More revenue does not imply a higher payment ratio

Now fix the proportional rule: the assessed amount is 160 in A and 40 in B. By the same reference date, matched payments are 120 and 36. Assume each amount concerns the same period and obligation.

Compare calculations and interpretation

A collected more units (120 versus 36), but B paid a larger fraction of its assessment (90% versus 75%). The combined ratio is (120 + 36) / (160 + 40) = 78%, not the unweighted average 82.5%. The weights are the assessed amounts.

A payment ratio does not establish full compliance: an assessment may omit unregistered taxpayers or unreported income. Timing, matching and measurement also matter. A shortfall alone does not establish intention.

Bridge to E-tivity 2: the unit, variable, reference period and denominator are part of the result. The 24-firm dataset in E-tivity 2 is a separate synthetic example.

SECTION 06 / 8

From a register to an audit-risk model

A tax administration wants to prioritise filings for human review. Its training data contain outcomes only for previously audited firms. Past audits concentrated on selected sectors.

Questions inherited from registers

  • Who is included, and who is absent?
  • What does each category or amount mean?
  • For what administrative purpose was it recorded?

Questions for an AI application

  • How were audit cases selected?
  • What exactly is the prediction target?
  • Who reviews errors and affected cases?
After writing: points to consider

Recorded audit findings reveal selected cases. An unaudited outcome is unobserved, not automatically a compliant case. Historical selection can shape the learned patterns. Check coverage and label definitions, evaluate the model on relevant new cases, and consider the consequences of false alarms and missed cases. A risk score supports review; it does not establish an intentional violation.
