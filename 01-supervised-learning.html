Part 01

Purpose and preparation

This activity connects the descriptive and inferential tools you already know with the task of predicting outcomes for new observations. You will define a learning problem, inspect how a model uses information, compare models of different complexity, and design an evaluation that resembles future use.

Allow 60–90 minutes. Work through the browser activity first. The R laboratory is a separate practical session; you do not need R to complete this activity. Prerequisites are means, variability, scatterplots and the distinction between a sample and a population.

Your final product: a short modelling proposal specifying the unit, target, prediction time, available predictors, split and evaluation measure.

Part 02

Identify the learning problem

In supervised learning, each training row contains predictors X and a known outcome Y. The outcome can be quantitative, such as travel time, or categorical, such as whether a delivery is late. Classification can involve more than two classes. Unsupervised learning uses predictors without a designated outcome label, for example to group similar records.

The number used to store a variable does not determine its statistical meaning. Coding “late” as 1 and “on time” as 0 does not turn a categorical target into a continuous quantity.

Part 03

Prediction, explanation and intervention

A predictive question asks whether available information helps forecast a new outcome. An associational question asks how observed outcomes differ with predictor values. A causal question asks what would happen if an intervention changed something.

Suppose students who study more tend to obtain higher scores. Study hours may help predict scores, but the regression slope is not automatically the effect of making any particular student study an extra hour. Prior knowledge, course difficulty and measurement error may also matter.

Similarly, an association between use of tax support and timely filing does not by itself measure the effect of offering support. People who seek support may differ from those who do not.

A model predicts late filing well using previous late filings. Which claim follows?

Part 04

Available information and leakage

A useful model must operate with information genuinely available at the prediction time. For a prediction at dispatch, actual arrival time is unavailable. Including it would allow the model to use the answer it is supposed to predict.

Leakage can also occur during data preparation. If a missing-value replacement, scaling rule or variable-selection rule is learned using the test set, the evaluation no longer represents a completely new dataset. The same problem arises when duplicated or related records are split across subsets.

Part 05

Separate fitting, selection and evaluation

Training data estimate model parameters. Validation data support decisions such as choosing a polynomial degree or a classification threshold. Test data evaluate the resulting frozen procedure. In the larger datasets supplied here, a fixed split column assigns 60% to training, 20% to validation and 20% to testing.

There is no universal best split ratio. With limited data, cross-validation can use development data more efficiently. With repeated observations or a changing environment, a random row split may answer the wrong question.

Future useAppropriate consideration
New independent cases from a stable populationA random split may be reasonable
New people, with several records per personKeep each person in one subset
Next year’s recordsTrain on earlier periods and evaluate later periods

Part 06

Loss functions and baselines

A loss function defines what it means to predict badly. Squared error strongly penalises large numerical misses; absolute error is less sensitive to extreme misses. For probabilities, log loss and Brier score compare the predicted chance with the event actually observed.

RMSE = √mean[(y − ŷ)²]    MAE = mean[|y − ŷ|]

A baseline provides a meaningful comparison. Use the training mean for constant squared-error prediction, the training median for absolute-error prediction, or the training event rate for constant probability prediction.

Part 08

Cross-validation and the full procedure

In 5-fold cross-validation, split the development data into five groups. Fit on four groups and predict the fifth; rotate the held-out group until each development observation has an out-of-fold prediction. Aggregate errors across these predictions to compare candidate procedures.

The procedure includes data preparation as well as the model. Scaling, imputation, feature screening and resampling must be fitted within the training portion of each fold. After selection, refit the chosen procedure on development data and evaluate on a separate test set.

In 04_validation_and_cv.R, the original training and validation rows are combined into an 80% development set. Five-fold cross-validation happens inside that set. The predefined test rows remain untouched until the final evaluation.

Part 09

Algorithms and human decisions

Linear and logistic regression are supervised learning methods. Nearest neighbours use nearby observations; trees partition the predictor space; random forests combine many trees; boosting builds a sequence of models; neural networks learn compositions of transformations. Greater complexity is not a substitute for a well-defined target and credible evaluation.

For a late-filing support programme, consider whether recorded labels cover every taxpayer or only selected cases. Historical selection can make the observed outcomes unrepresentative. A model may learn the past selection process as well as the target of interest.

Also ask who experiences false alarms and missed cases, whether error rates are stable across groups, and how a person can question or correct a prediction. These are part of the modelling problem, not a consequence of a single accuracy number.

A high AUC or low RMSE alone establishes that a model is appropriate for deployment.

Part 10

Final task and R bridge

Prepare a 300–500 word modelling proposal. Choose delivery delays, exam scores or late filing. Specify: (1) unit and target; (2) prediction time and three candidate features; (3) a leakage risk; (4) split design; (5) baseline and metric; (6) one limitation and a human decision the model could support.

Assessment focuses on whether the question is precise, the split matches future use, evaluation is independent of selection, and interpretation recognises limitations. A technically complex algorithm is not required.
